How AeroGate works

AeroGate assigns every flight at Toronto Pearson to a gate, keeps that plan valid as delays, gate closures, aircraft swaps and diversions arrive, and explains each decision. At every update it solves the whole remaining day exactly, and shows the result on a map of the real terminal.

Why it beats today's tools

  • Fewer passengers stuck on the tarmac. On the Pearson evening, with the same storm, jetbridge failure, diversion and swap, greedy rules (keep gates, take the first good free gate) leave 11 flights holding for 320 min, about 1,320 passenger-hours. AeroGate looks at the whole evening at once: 3 holds, 70 min, about 234 passenger-hours (82% fewer), for a 20% lower score.
  • Never an illegal action. Every action is re-checked with the evaluator's own rules before it is sent. On 40 random disrupted days AeroGate fails once (unavoidable), the provided greedy 3 times and first-fit 6 times.
  • Best score on the challenge. 96.2 on the 12 challenge scenarios against 104.3 (greedy) and 96.8 (first-fit); best on 26 of 33 random days.
  • Every change explained. Each decision lists the rules it passed, what it costs and why every other gate lost, so a planner can approve it in seconds.
  • Re-plans in about a second. Each re-plan after the first is proven optimal in at most 1.5 s, so it keeps up with the operation.

Each update, step by step

  1. Read the updateNew flights, delays, cancellations, equipment swaps, gate outages and diversions arrive as JSON messages.
  2. Build optionsEvery flight and gate pair that passes the hard rules becomes a yes/no option. Pairs that break a rule are never created, so the solver cannot pick them.
  3. OptimiseGoogle OR-Tools CP-SAT picks one option per flight to minimise the evaluator's own score. Small days solve to a proven optimum in milliseconds.
  4. Order the movesMoves are applied one at a time, so a flight can only move onto a gate after the flight holding it has left. Swaps that cannot be applied are ruled out inside the model.
  5. Safety replayEvery action is re-checked with the evaluator's own rule code before it is returned. If anything fails: greedy plan, then the reference solver, then no change. It never crashes.
  6. ExplainEach decision records the rules it passed, what it costs and why every other gate lost. Click any aircraft to see it.

The optimisation model

Decision: x[f, g] = 1 when flight f uses gate g (with a tarmac-hold length added for Pearson).

The Pearson map

  • The map is OpenStreetMap; gates, stands, runways and taxiways come from the same data, including dual-named swing gates such as E67/F67.
  • Pearson's usual west-flow setup: land on 24R and 24L, depart from 23. Each flight lands on whichever runway gets it to its gate sooner, spaced by wake category (3 NM; 4 NM heavy behind heavy; 5 NM medium behind heavy), and taxis on the shortest real taxi route to the stand AeroGate assigned it.
  • Arrivals from 24L hold short of 24R (red stop bars) until the last 24R landing has turned off and the next is more than 2 NM out.
  • Departures push back, queue at the 23 holding point and take off 1.5 min apart (2 min behind a heavy).
  • Taxiing is simulated every 3 seconds: an aircraft never drives into the one ahead and at crossings the later aircraft gives way. Over the whole evening no two moving aircraft come within 39 m.
  • An aircraft that must wait for its gate (a planned tarmac hold, or its stand is still taken) taxis to a holding spot on an unused taxiway and waits there, not on the apron.
  • At each plan update, amber arrows show every gate change; click one for the reason.
  • Aircraft are drawn at true size when zoomed in. Zoomed out they are drawn larger so they stay visible, but never so large that two icons overlap.
  • Gates & resources tab: every arrival in the next hour with its planned gate and the best other legal gates (extra points if moved there). Pick a flight to colour its gates on the map: green planned, blue legal but costs more, grey taken.
  • Ground crew and baggage per pier (purple badges at each pier's baggage area, shown with the Gates & resources tab) are planning estimates the planner does not use: ramp crews D 14, F 6, E 16, cargo 2, two per widebody turn; baggage belts of 1,200 bags/h (D 2, F 1, E 4).

Stand sizes, walking distances, crew counts and baggage numbers are estimates. Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors.
